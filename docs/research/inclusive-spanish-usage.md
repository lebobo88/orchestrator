## Scope and framing

This note covers two analytically distinct questions about inclusive and neutral Spanish/Hispanic-identity usage. Content authors must keep them separate:

1. Contested gender-neutral Spanish morphology (the -e/elle/todes/@/x family of endings) versus RAE/FundéuRAE-compatible neutral strategies (collective nouns, abstract nouns, rephrasing, moderate desdoblamiento).
2. The Latino/Latina/Latinx/Latine group-ethnonym question, which concerns English-language and bilingual US-Hispanic self-identification, not Spanish grammatical morphology.

This note is strictly descriptive. It documents institutional positions, usage data, and open contradictions as reported; it does not take a position on which form is correct or preferred, and no claim here should be read as advocacy.

## 1. Contested morphology versus RAE/FundéuRAE-compatible strategies

**Contested tier.** Endings such as -e (elle, todes), @, and x are regionally variable, register-marked as informal or activist rather than standard usage, and are formally banned for official/administrative use in some target-market jurisdictions (see the Argentina contradiction below). The curriculum architecture document's design-system badge example, "amigos/amigues," is an example from this contested tier.

**RAE/FundéuRAE-compatible tier.** Collective or epicene nouns ("la ciudadanía," "el personal," "las personas," "el estudiantado"), abstract nouns, rephrasing to avoid gendered agreement, and moderate desdoblamiento ("los niños y las niñas") used sparingly. FundéuRAE, the RAE-affiliated Spanish-usage authority, treats desdoblamiento as one moderate option to be used sparingly and recommends collective/abstract nouns as its preferred general alternative. This tier is reported as portable across all ten of the curriculum's locations and unaffected by the administrative bans on -e/@/x described below.

**Approved pedagogical instruction (carried into the curriculum architecture document).** Teach the RAE/FundéuRAE-compatible tier as active, productive vocabulary, especially for business and formal register. Teach the contested tier for recognition and regional/register awareness only; never present a contested form as standard or universally correct.

## 2. Institutional positions

- **RAE.** Maintains that the generic masculine is grammatically correct and inclusive; rejects "-e" as a neutral gender marker; has characterized many inclusive-language proposals as ideologically motivated [S1]. (RAE's own fuller "Informe... sobre el lenguaje inclusivo" report, S2, could not be retrieved; this position is sourced via RAE's shorter official note, S1, and press reporting, S3, instead.) RAE briefly added "elle" to its Observatorio de Palabras and then removed it, reported as an effort to avoid the appearance of institutional endorsement [S3]. (Reported via Spanish-language press, including El Universal, El Debate, and TheObjective, 2024-2025.)
- **FundéuRAE.** See the RAE/FundéuRAE-compatible tier above; its guidance is the RAE-affiliated basis for treating collective/abstract nouns and sparing desdoblamiento as the safe general path [S4].
- **Instituto Cervantes.** Takes a more permissive pedagogical stance than RAE. Its director has publicly favored symmetrical double-plural forms (for example "los niños y las niñas") and has publicly criticized RAE's position [S5].
- **Spain's Ministry of Justice.** Published an official "Guía para el uso de un lenguaje más inclusivo e igualitario" (2023), which emphasizes non-sexist symmetrical forms rather than "-e" morphology [S9].

**Flagged contradiction 1 (Spain, institutional): RAE versus Instituto Cervantes.** These two Spanish-government-adjacent language authorities disagree publicly, and that disagreement became visible through late 2025 [S5], consistent with S3's report of RAE's "elle" reversal. This is documented as an open, unresolved institutional split, not a position this note or the curriculum document adjudicates.

## 3. Regional government positions and usage

- **Argentina.** The national government banned "-e," "@," "x," and "unnecessary" feminine forms in public-administration documents (February 2024) [S6]. The City of Buenos Aires separately banned these forms in schools (Circular 4/2022), a measure since challenged in court [S7]. (S7's sources date to the 2022 announcement itself; they are used here for historical/structural grounding of an ongoing policy, not as a claim about current 2026 acceptance or enforcement status.) Despite these bans, "-e"/"todes" usage is documented as highest in the Southern Cone, especially Argentina, driven by youth and social-media use since the 2018 feminist wave [S12].

**Flagged contradiction 2 (Argentina, official-versus-community): the ban on -e/@/x in public administration and schools coexists with the highest reported community and youth usage of those same forms in the region.** This is documented as an open contradiction between official policy [S6][S7] and community practice [S12], not a claim that the ban has suppressed usage or that usage undermines the ban's legitimacy.

- **Mexico.** Multiple federal and state institutions, including the Secretaría de Relaciones Exteriores and the Suprema Corte de Justicia de la Nación, publish official "lenguaje incluyente y no sexista" guides that treat inclusive language as an institutional and judicial expectation, the opposite posture from Argentina's administrative ban [S8]. "Todes"/"-e" usage is present and rising among Mexican youth and media, though still contested [S12].

## 4. Regional confidence and coverage

Strong, location-specific evidence exists for Mexico, Argentina, and Spain (institutional positions and the two contradictions above), and for the US-Latino ethnonym question (Section 5). For the remaining six of the curriculum's ten required locations, Colombia, Peru, the Dominican Republic, El Salvador, Honduras, and Ecuador, the evidence base rests only on general Latin-America-wide trend data, not location-specific institutional or usage findings. Content authors writing inclusive-language dialect notes for these six locations should label that content as lower-confidence and general-regional, not present it with the same certainty as the Mexico, Argentina, and Spain treatment above.

## 5. The Latino/Latina/Latinx/Latine ethnonym question (kept separate from morphology)

This question concerns how US Hispanic/Latino communities describe themselves in English-language and bilingual contexts. It is most directly relevant to the curriculum's US Spanish zone and to the heritage-learner track (Phase 8), not to the ten core dialect locations' Spanish-grammar content.

Per Pew Research Center's National Survey of Latinos (fielded November 6-19, 2023, n = 5,078 US Hispanic adults) [S10]:

- 47% have heard of "Latinx" (up from 23% in 2019), but only 4% use it, and 75% of those aware of the term say it should not be used.
- 18% have heard of "Latine."
- 81% prefer "Hispanic" or "Latino"; only 3% prefer "Latinx" or "Latine."
- Awareness and use of both "Latinx" and "Latine" skew young, college-educated, and toward LGB respondents.

Per an Axios-Ipsos/Telemundo poll, 41% of US Latinos report being comfortable with "Latine" [S11]. "Latine" is increasingly reported as preferred over "Latinx" specifically because the "-e" ending fits Spanish phonology (parallel to existing words such as "estudiantes"), while "-x" is criticized as an English-language imposition that is difficult to pronounce in Spanish [S11]. "Latine" is reported to have originated within Spanish-speaking nonbinary and feminist communities [S11].

Content should not present "Latinx" or "Latine" as the community-preferred or standard term: the sourced data shows low use and mixed-to-negative reception for "Latinx" specifically, alongside majority preference for "Hispanic" or "Latino." This question should be presented as documented, evolving, and contested, not settled.
